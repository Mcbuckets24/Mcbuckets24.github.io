Meaning in the Presence of Absence

04.05.26

June 19th, 2019, YouTube streamer Desmond Amofah, known to fans as Etika, tragically took his own life. For his global community of fans, it was devastating. A champion of positivity and energy. To see, what was to many, a beacon of hope pass on in such a preventable manner, left the community void. So many were quick to jump to blame. The people he knew, the things the community had said, his mental illness, his social media presence. All of it. But as the immediate grief ultimately settled, the wider network of blame slowly funneled into one idea: In combination with his overlooked mental illness, Etika’s online image, channel, chat, and persona was the primary point of blame. As he himself said, he was “too scared to drop my image.”

For many of us, myself included, Etika was the first person we lost to suicide. Although we didn’t know him personally, his infectious personality and kind nature led us to view him as an idol. Having been deeply impacted by his death, many in the community looked further into suicide and how they could make efforts to implement future prevention measures to prevent a repetition of Etika’s story. The more we learned about Etika and his personal story, the more we realized how he was only the beginning of a larger story. Since the 2000s, suicide rates have steadily increased in the United States. In conjunction with Etika’s story it suggests a dangerous possibility. The largest societal change since the early 2000s was the introduction of the internet and social media platforms. Were these platforms behind the upward trend of suicides? I think we’ve all heard the narrative before, the damaging mental health effects of the internet, the way it changes how we think, the way we perceive ourselves, and the way we perceive others. Social media seemed like the obvious answer, the reason that we had all been searching for. The thing to act upon. A root at the seemingly unsolvable future that faced us.

The adage that social media is bad for you is seemingly accepted in today’s world, but is it the entire truth? Is it supported by data? If we, as a society, are going to assign blame and correspondingly identify corrective actions, it’s imperative that we ensure our diagnosis is correct. This felt like something to hold onto, a possible path forward for the many not only in Etika’s community, but for those of us left in the shadows of loved ones or acquaintances who have taken their lives. It’s so easy to be consumed by the absence of those we lost, but with something to act upon, a chance is provided to make this world a little less lonely, a little more loving, and a little brighter for everyone fighting out there. It’s all I really desired and still do. The chance to make a difference for someone, to make even a single person think twice. That feels like everything.

So, returning to the core of our problem, we need to assess the trend as a whole. Checking the data itself, I uncovered that the National Center for Health Statistics reported “The total age-adjusted suicide rate increased 30% from 2002 (10.9 deaths per 100,000 standard population) … to 14.2 in 2022” (Garnett). The data represented an irrefutable truth, for one reason or another, there’s been a distinct and dramatic increase in suicides in the past 20 years. While merely a confirmation of what we already knew, seeing this fact in numbers creates a feeling of crushing hopelessness. A thirty percent increase is no coincidence. Looking at it from above, it feels as if there must be a specific cause. I, almost in an act of fear, turned to what the community had believed to be a primary cause, what the world as a whole seemed to accept as the cause, social media and the internet. What I found wasn’t the clear-cut solution it seemed from the surface.

I looked toward any direct correlation between social media and mental health. In Osman Ulvi’s analysis of mental health and social media use, I found what we all know: “excessive use of social media can be related to depression and anxiety” (Ulvi 1). In our world, this feels like an apparent truth. There are many stories of people being profoundly and negatively impacted by their time on the internet. Those who fell into the easy trap of social comparison, addictive algorithms, and the isolating nature of always carrying around an infinite expanse of entertainment and knowledge. While I know this, it’s hard for me to simply accept this at face value. For me, the internet has had an immensely positive effect on my mental health over the years. Through the internet, I was able to find myself in online communities that I felt better represented who I wanted to be more than my immediate environment. I’ve found so many people that I love. People that’ve changed my life forever. Looking back, the internet provided me with the people, communities, and conversations that’ve made me into who I am today. Without the internet and social media, none of that would’ve ever happened. I wouldn’t be the person I’m glad to be today. I’ve found homes for the struggles I’ve undergone. People that shared my experience were willing to not only talk but listen. People who, in the long run, saved me.

It’s as Ulvi notes, many social media sites had “been used to benefit mental health by bringing people of similar mental health situations together and creating a supportive environment” (Ulvi 23). This was combatting the common narrative that surrounds social media but aligning with what I know to be true. Being able to share struggles with others, learning that we’re not alone in our challenges by connecting with others online, can be a blessing. Social media, while easy to think of the malicious ways it can be used, is a double-edged sword. For every story of harm, there are countless stories of connection and support. “Although these platforms can be a distorted reality for some, they ultimately still serve as platforms where individuals can express themselves” (Ulvi 23). Expression, at its core, is what humans are about. When given the ability to express oneself without judgment or doubt from those around them, many of us find a kind of haven in online spaces, an escape from the realities that often lead people toward suicidal thoughts. While it would be easy to say that the internet and social media are net positives and therefore not to blame, that ignores the complexity of the issue.

In viewing the internet as more of a site of expression and safety than one of unpredictability, I looked further into what I saw as a possible next step. Ulvi mentioned the “untapped potential for early detection using social media platforms,” or intervention through online spaces (Ulvi 23). I was immediately drawn to this idea. In the wake of Etika’s death, much of my recovery was done through finding and interacting with his online community. Finding others struggling in the same way I was felt like hope. It made me realize I wasn't alone. And I don't think I'm unique in that. Nada Abou Seif at Cambridge found the same thing playing out in research studying peer support for self-harm, he concluded “Although I understand the high-risk nature of peer support in increasing some self-harm behaviours, I consider the benefits to outweigh the cons in terms of accessing support and generating a sense of self-empowerment and online community presence for young people” (Seif 13). I think this is what lies at the core of this issue. The internet, social media, and the connections it offers are inherently high-risk. For some like me, it pays off hugely in the long term. Building community and enabling people to find their voice and help where they might have not otherwise. But for others, it exemplifies the opposite. A deep hole of negativity and hatred. It’s always waiting there, but it’s up to the user if that’s something they want to engage in. That’s the cost of the internet. With its usage being so wide and freely accessible it presents an infinite number of paths to someone every time they log on. Where they end up isn’t something that can be determined at the beginning. So, what does that mean? I agree with Seif in his claims that the benefits outweigh the negatives, but that doesn’t eliminate the costs of those negatives and the situations they’ve placed us in today. How are we supposed to reckon with that?

But maybe it didn't have to be so unstructured. I started looking into whether there were more deliberate ways the internet was being used to reach people before it was too late. This feels like a strong compromise. For those of us who find the positives in the freedom of the internet, we can go and have our fun, but for those of us who have negative experiences, there’s still a framework for recovery. Something not too different from what they were initially seeking in the first place. It’s not perfect, but it’s far too much to ask for perfect at this point. It was a starting point, and if that’s all we can get, then maybe that’s all we really need. I looked to research by Fatemeh Abbasi on these structured intervention communities to find, “certain digital interventions, particularly guided self-help programs and multi-component approaches, showed potential in alleviating suicidal ideation” (Abbasi 16). This offers a possible path forward. If the internet not only provides people with a sense of community but also has the potential to intervene for those struggling with suicidal ideation, it begins to feel like an obvious direction for prevention. What initially resembled the core problem began to resemble part of the solution. Yet things are not that simple. The study acknowledges that, despite their potential, “internet-based interventions show inconsistent effects on reducing suicidal ideation” (Abbasi 1). While some interventions show notable impact, they cannot account for everyone. Most people are either unaware, not willing, or disinterested in this type of program. They go about their normal lives on the internet, looking for whatever interest brought them there today, not to seek out help for the very problems their screen might be enabling. While it’s promising that these resources exist and continue to improve, this can’t excuse the tens of thousands of suicides in the US every year. It all seemed like a dead end. For some of us, the internet was hope, for others it was the direct opposite, that didn’t feel like a solution. That’s not something to act upon. I don’t know if there’s any hope to be found in that.

If the internet cannot be solely blamed for the rise in suicide rates, it suggests a reassessment of the problem at large. What else has changed to result in these statistics? Looking closer revealed what I learned to be a deeper systemic issue: in the past two decades, men have died from suicide at a rate almost four times that of women. That’s no coincidence. The internet felt like half of the puzzle, and this seemed like the rest. Tania L. King’s study into the relationship between expressions of masculinity and suicidal ideation in young males offered more insight into the disparity. King and their team found through using a personality inventory and survey that “greater conformity to violent norms and self-reliance norms was associated with higher odds of reporting suicidal ideation” (King 1). The stigmatized path that the general society pushes men to follow often leads those same men down dangerous paths of self-isolation, self reliance, and in the worst case, suicidal thoughts. Despite progress in the acceptance of mental illness, the numbers don’t reflect the same progression. If our society is supposedly becoming more accepting and open, then why aren’t the men that live within it feeling the same? As a male myself being born in the mid-2000s, I can speak to the fact that while therapy is more widely encouraged and people claim to be more accepting, there is still that air of judgement around admitting you’re struggling mentally as a man. King observed that “Mental health stigma is known to be a barrier to help-seeking… for young men, this is perhaps more keenly experienced because poor mental health, and help-seeking are both at odds with their internalized masculine norms” (King 6). So even if vocally, and with the use of the internet, people like to champion this era as the most accepting and open of all, the core norms and stigmas at the root of our systems have not gone anywhere. Which leads to this sort of imbalance that I think is at the core of this issue. The internet has allowed all people, men included, to enjoy a freedom of expression and ability to express distress or need for help, but the larger structure has not taught them how to process or understand those feelings. This creates a discrepancy between the ability to express distress and the ability to handle it. This is an issue that I think becomes a large contributor to the trend at the heart of our problem. This brings us closer to the root of the issue. Rather than a single cause, the rise in suicide rates may reflect a deeper tension between increased visibility of distress and a lack of meaningful change in how that distress is addressed.

So, having found even the smallest part of the puzzle, I return to what spun me to search for these answers in the first place, and I find that I have not been completely honest about myself. Etika’s death seven years ago changed me forever, but four months ago, I lost a friend to suicide as well. Which leads me to say that even if I’d like to pinpoint some reason at the core of this issue, something we could eradicate forever and run around free of it ever occurring again, I know that’s not the truth. Because suicide isn’t a puzzle to be solved like I wish it was. It’s so much more complex than that. In the face of the failure that was the seven years of my life dedicated to never letting another person die in that way again, I learned something invaluable. I learned that meaning isn’t always what we did or what we tried, sometimes meaning is what we became after. Sometimes meaning is the person we try to be tomorrow. I think our system today not only fails those who need help, but those who are left behind. I find that in writing this, I’m a reflection of that reality, because as much as I’d like to say that this is a wholehearted attempt at researching and trying to find some cause for the suicides that’ve occurred in my life, it’s more of my way of coping with it all. Of finally being honest with myself. For so long, I tried to save everyone, but sadly, I learned that’s not possible. While I initially felt that made my life’s mission pointless, a friend told me something simple that helped change my mind, “it's way easier to see when your efforts don't work than when they do, because when it does work, the status quo is maintained; people are still here, and they still go about their normal lives.” It’s so easy to get wrapped up in the result, but the story along the way is just as important. There are still so many of us here. Even if I don’t have the solution to quelling the upward trend of suicide rates, I do know what saved me. And maybe, somewhere, that can help someone else too. It’s impossible to try and fix everything, what we can do is something much simpler.

While remaining cognizant of the issue at large and using our collective voices to fight against and change the rotten system that led us to the world we live in today, that’s not the only way we can make a difference. Because we may look at decades long graphs or read 20-page longitudinal studies and feel discouraged, but the reality in front of us is not drawn by those pens. We’re the ones who get to write that reality every day. I think that’s the biggest blessing we have as humans. What we instead choose to do is to keep the data in the back of our minds and push the very life we live with those around us to the front. We are sometimes blinded by grief and the permeance of death in its wake, but who would we be to ignore the effect we still have on everyone around us? There are still so many people that we love to support. By being just the slightest bit kinder, more caring, and more loving, each and every day, we find that when ten years pass in the way ten years do, we’ll find ourselves so much better off than we were when we began, unsure of where we’ll end, but sure that wherever it may be, we’ll still be free to share the love that keeps our world alive. Shoya Ishida, the protagonist of my favorite manga, A Silent Voice, said, “There are some things you just can’t change. I know that all too well. I think it’s the time you spend trying to change that’s more important” (Oima 7). I think that’s the heart of this all. We can’t change the past, but we can change tomorrow.

This essay’s first draft began as a letter. As I bring it to a close now, I feel like it has almost retaken that shape. Not as one to my mother as it was previously, but as one to me. A letter that maybe I felt I needed. One that acknowledges how hard and scary life can be, but how beautiful the love we share for one another is in spite of that. It’s okay to lose and grieve. It’s okay to cry. All we can do is carry on the spirit of that which was lost in hopes to make a more positive future for the rest of the world still trudging on. I have no solution, but we still have each other, and I think that’s all we really need.
